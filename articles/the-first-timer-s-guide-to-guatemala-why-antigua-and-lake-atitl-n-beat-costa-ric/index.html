When I tell people I spent two weeks exploring a stunning Central American destination complete with volcanoes, coffee farms, colonial architecture, and deep blue lakes for less than fifty dollars a day, they almost always guess Costa Rica.

They are almost always wrong.

While Costa Rica has built an incredible tourism marketing machine over the last thirty years, it has also priced itself out of true budget travel. If you want tropical beauty, outdoor adventure, and rich culture without draining your savings account, Guatemala is the smarter choice. Specifically, flying into Guatemala City, heading straight to the colonial city of Antigua, and then moving on to Lake Atitlán offers a masterclass in affordable travel.

Here is how I broke down my expenses, why these two spots beat Costa Rica on price, and how you can plan your own trip.

The Cost Reality: Guatemala vs. Costa Rica

Let’s look at the numbers. In Costa Rica, a standard bed in a clean hostel dorm frequently runs thirty to forty-five dollars a night in peak season. A basic meal at a local soda costs ten to fifteen dollars, and guided excursions easily top seventy-five dollars.

In Guatemala, my private room in a boutique-style hostel in Antigua cost twenty-two dollars a night. A hearty breakfast of eggs, black beans, fried plantains, and handmade corn tortillas at a local market ran me about three dollars.

Transportation is another massive win for Guatemala. Because the country is geographically compact and lacks the sprawling highway toll systems found elsewhere in the region, getting around is remarkably cheap. Public “chicken buses” cost pocket change, though as a first-timer, you will likely prefer the shared tourist shuttles. These vans run between Antigua and Lake Atitlán multiple times a day and cost around fifteen to twenty dollars total for a three-hour journey.

Antigua: Cobblestones, Volcanoes, and Cheap Culture

Antigua is the ideal soft landing for a first-time international traveler. Located about forty-minues from the Guatemala City airport, this UNESCO World Heritage city sits in a valley surrounded by three towering volcanoes.

What makes Antigua a budget traveler’s dream is that your biggest daily expenses are essentially zero. You do not need to book expensive city tours because the main activity is simply walking.

  • Wander the ruins: Many of the 16th-century convents and churches damaged by historical earthquakes are open for a tiny entry fee or free to view from the street.
  • Eat at the Mercado Central: Skip the trendy cafes on your first day and head to the municipal market behind the bus terminal. You can sit at a communal counter and eat pepian (a rich, traditional meat stew) for less than four dollars.
  • Hike Cerro de la Cruz: This scenic overlook sits on a hill overlooking the city and the massive Volcán de Agua. It is completely free to hike up, and local police patrol the path, making it safe during daylight hours.

If you want to tackle an active volcano like Acatenango—where you camp overnight and watch neighboring Fuego erupt glowing red lava—booking a local guide in Antigua will cost roughly forty to sixty dollars total, including park fees, a guide, and rented gear. A comparable guided multi-day trek in Costa Rica easily starts at two hundred dollars.

Lake Atitlán: The High-Altitude Escape

After a few days in Antigua, take a morning shuttle to Lake Atitlán. Formed inside a massive volcanic crater, the lake is ringed by a dozen different villages, each with its own distinct personality.

Accommodation prices vary by town, but they consistently undercut similar lakeside or beachfront spots in Central America.

  • San Marcos La Laguna: Known for its yoga studios, holistic healing centers, and bohemian vibe. Hostels here range from twenty to thirty dollars a night for dorms.
  • Panajachel: The main commercial hub. It is the easiest to reach from Antigua and offers the cheapest street food and souvenir shopping, though it feels a bit busier and less secluded.
  • San Juan La Laguna: A quieter, highly walkable village famous for its art cooperatives, textile weavers, and organic coffee farms.

Getting between these villages is half the fun and remarkably inexpensive. Instead of driving, you take public wooden motorboats known as lanchas. Hopping from Panajachel to San Juan takes about thirty minutes and costs roughly twenty-five quetzales (about three US dollars).

You can spend days swimming off docks, hiking the Indian Nose trail for sunrise, or taking Spanish immersion classes that cost around seven dollars an hour for one-on-one instruction—a fraction of what you would pay for language schools in Spain or Costa Rica.

Smart Logistics for Your First Trip

To keep your trip smooth and budget-friendly, keep a few practical logistics in mind:

  1. Book your airport transfer in advance: Do not hail a random taxi outside the Guatemala City airport. Ask your Antigua hostel or hotel to arrange an official driver to pick you up. It should cost around thirty-five to forty dollars total, which you can split if you are traveling with a partner.
  2. Carry small bills: While US dollars are sometimes accepted in tourist hubs, you will get terrible exchange rates. Use local ATMs to withdraw Guatemalan Quetzales, but try to break large 100-quetzal notes ($13 USD) whenever you buy a small item like water or a pastry. Street vendors rarely have change for large bills.
  3. Tap water is not safe: Budget travel means avoiding stomach bugs. Always drink filtered or bottled water, and use it to brush your teeth. Most hostels provide free filtered water refills, so pack a reusable metal water bottle.

Choosing Guatemala over Costa Rica doesn’t mean compromising on safety, scenery, or comfort. It simply means your dollar stretches twice as far, leaving you room to stay a few days longer or splurge on a guided volcano hike without checking your bank account.

As you start pulling together your packing list, flight dates, and hostel bookmarks for your Guatemalan itinerary, consider how you want to handle your day-to-day spending and booking logistics to keep the trip as seamless as possible.
